Your first crypto payment as a Nigerian creator

A client wants to pay in USDT. Here is exactly what happens, what to send them, and how the naira ends up in a wallet you can actually spend.

You finish a job. The client is in London, or Dubai, or somewhere your bank does not like. They ask: can I pay in USDT? You say yes because saying no means waiting another two weeks for a wire that might bounce. Then you sit there wondering what you just agreed to.

This is the first-payment guide we wish someone had handed us. No theory. Just what to send, what to check, and how the money becomes naira you can spend.

What you actually need

You need an address on the right network, a way to convert the crypto without hunting a P2P buyer, and a naira balance you can send to a bank or pay a bill from. That is the whole stack. Everything else is decoration.

  • A receive address generated for the asset they are sending: USDT, USDC, BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB, or TRX.
  • The network written next to that address. USDT on Tron is not USDT on Ethereum.
  • A conversion that credits naira to your main wallet automatically, not a second crypto balance you then have to cash out.

What to send the client

Do not send a screenshot of a random exchange deposit screen. Send one thing: a TranxactPay invoice, or a receive address with the network named in the same message. Example: “Please send 120 USDT on TRC-20 to this address. Any other network and the funds may be lost.”

If they are paying for a specific job, use an invoice link with the amount locked. If they tip you often, a Tip Me link that stays open is cleaner. Either way they pay from their wallet. You do not give them your bank details.

The part that used to hurt

On a typical P2P app the crypto lands, then you list naira for sale, then a stranger chats you, then you argue about rate, then you wait for them to release. That is four extra steps after you already did the work.

On Tranxact the inbound payment converts as it arrives. The naira hits your main balance. From there you send to any Nigerian bank, pay airtime or electricity, or transfer to another Tranxact user for free. No buyer. No chat.

The one mistake that cannot be undone

Wrong network. If they send USDT on ERC-20 to a TRC-20 address, those funds are usually gone. Confirm the network on your receive screen, write it in the message, and do not accept “I already sent it” as a substitute for checking. Blockchain payments do not reverse.

Do that once, correctly, and the second payment is boring. That is the point.

USDT vs USDC: which stablecoin should you accept?

Both are dollars on-chain. The network, the issuer, and who is paying you decide which one you should actually ask for.

Clients will offer whichever stablecoin their wallet already holds. Your job is not to win an internet argument about Tether. It is to get paid without losing money to the wrong network or a surprise conversion.

The short version

  • USDT is what most African and Asian clients already have. Tron (TRC-20) is the cheap, common rail.
  • USDC is what a lot of US and European clients hold, usually on Ethereum or Solana.
  • For a Nigerian creator, accept both. Do not make the client buy a coin they do not have.

Where the money actually moves

USDT on Tron is popular because fees are low and almost every P2P desk in Lagos already speaks it. USDC on Solana is fast and cheap. USDC or USDT on Ethereum works, but the gas can eat a small invoice. If a client insists on ERC-20 for a ₦40k job, the fee may be a real percentage of the payment.

Tranxact accepts USDT and USDC (alongside BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB, and TRX) and converts inbound payments to naira automatically. You do not keep a dollar balance you then have to sell.

What to put on the invoice

Name the asset and the network. “120 USDT, TRC-20” is a complete instruction. “USDT” by itself is not. If you use a TranxactPay link, the receive screen already shows the network next to the address. Send them the link and the guesswork goes away.

If a regular client always pays the same coin, save that as their default. Switching networks mid-relationship is how wrong-chain transfers happen.

A note on “which is safer”

USDC is issued by Circle, a regulated US company. USDT is issued by Tether and is larger by circulation. For the size of invoice most freelancers take, issuer risk is not the thing that loses you money. Wrong network is. Ask for the coin the client already holds, on a network you have an address for, and convert to naira the moment it lands.

How to price your work in crypto without getting burned

Quote in naira, settle in crypto, and stop letting a six-hour BTC swing eat your fee.

You quoted $200 when BTC was one number. They paid three days later when it was another. You did the same work and took home less. That is not “the market.” That is a pricing bug.

Quote in naira. Accept crypto.

Your cost of living is naira. Food, data, rent, the generator. Price the job in ₦, then let the payment convert at the rate on the day it arrives. Do not lock a BTC amount on Monday for a transfer that happens Friday.

Stablecoins (USDT, USDC) remove most of that swing. If a client can pay in either, prefer that for anything that is not a large, immediate transfer. Bitcoin is fine when you are converting on arrival, not when the invoice sits unpaid for a week.

Put a validity window on the quote

A simple line on the invoice: “Amount locked for 48 hours. After that the naira total is recalculated.” That is normal in any business that deals with FX. You are not being difficult. You are not absorbing their delay for free.

  • Small jobs (under ₦100k): stablecoin, pay within 48 hours.
  • Retainers: monthly invoice in naira, paid in USDT/USDC.
  • Large one-offs: 50% to start, 50% on delivery, both converting on receipt.

Do not “hold for a better rate”

If the client paid you to do a job, that money is operating capital, not a trade. Convert on arrival. If you want to speculate, do it with money you can afford to watch. Mixing the two is how freelancers end up unable to pay rent after a green candle they did not sell.

Tranxact converts inbound crypto to naira automatically so the default is the professional one: get paid, see naira, spend. You can still send, pay bills, or withdraw to a bank from the same balance.

Fees belong on the quote

Network fees and conversion fees are real. If you swallow them silently, you discounted the job. Either gross up the invoice so the naira you need is what lands, or show the fee before the client confirms, which is how TranxactPay presents it. Hidden fees are how trust dies. Surprise shortfalls are how you work cheaper than you meant to.

What happens after a client pays you in Bitcoin

From the moment the transaction hits the chain to the moment naira is sitting in a wallet you can spend. A walkthrough, not a whitepaper.

Someone just sent you bitcoin. Congratulations. Also: it is not naira yet, it is not in your bank, and if you treat a block explorer screenshot as “paid in full” you will get hurt.

First, wait for confirmation

A Bitcoin payment is a transaction on a public chain. It is not done the second they hit send. For an invoice you care about, wait for confirmations before you deliver the files. Your wallet should show the payment as pending, then completed. Do not ship on “I have sent it” alone.

Then convert. Do not babysit a second balance.

The old path: BTC sits in an exchange or a P2P app. You list naira. A buyer chats you. You argue. Maybe it releases today. Maybe it does not.

The Tranxact path: inbound BTC is converted automatically and credited to your main naira wallet. Same wallet you send from, pay bills from, and receive other payments into. No separate “crypto tab” you have to remember to cash out.

Then spend it like naira, because it is

  • Send to any Nigerian bank account.
  • Pay airtime, data, electricity, TV, or betting from the app.
  • Transfer to another Tranxact user at ₦0.

That is the product. Crypto in, naira you can actually use. The ledger books the deposit, the conversion, and the spend the same way a bank would, so the balance you see is the balance that is there.

If the amount looks short

Check three things: the network fee the sender paid (that comes out of their side, not yours), the conversion fee shown before credit, and whether they sent the full invoice amount. If they underpaid, do not deliver the rest of the work until the remainder lands. A partial Bitcoin payment is not a rounding error. It is an unpaid invoice.

Once it is credited, treat it as naira. Send it, spend it, or leave it in the wallet. The bitcoin part of the story should already be over.
